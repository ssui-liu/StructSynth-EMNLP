Tabular data underpins high-stakes applications in healthcare~\cite{choi2017generating}, finance~\cite{rundo2019machine}, and education~\cite{luan2021review}, valued for the interpretable dependency relationships it encodes among features.
These dependencies are what enable Machine Learning (ML) models to learn meaningful decision boundaries, yet in many critical domains data scarcity poses a fundamental challenge~\cite{fonseca2023tabular}.
When available samples are severely limited ($n \le 100$), dependency discovery becomes unstable---statistical association tests lose power, graph learning algorithms produce unreliable structures, and deep generative models overfit to the few available records~\cite{chai2022data, borisov2022deep}.
Synthetic data generation offers a potential remedy, but effective synthesis must \textbf{preserve the dependency relationships} inherent in the original data rather than merely augmenting volume.

% Goal: Use Fig.1 as a method taxonomy showing three paradigms and their structural bottlenecks
To address data scarcity, conventional generative approaches have been developed with distinct strategies for handling feature dependencies (see Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}). \textbf{Deep Generative Models (DGMs)}, including Variational Autoencoders~\cite{patki2016synthetic}, Generative Adversarial Networks~\cite{xu2019modeling}, and Diffusion Models~\cite{kotelnikov2023tabddpm}, learn complex feature dependencies \textit{implicitly} by fitting the data distribution. However, this implicit dependency learning process is severely compromised when training data is sparse, causing the learned patterns to distort and generating low-fidelity synthetic samples. \textbf{Structure-Aware Methods} take a different approach by explicitly modeling the data's underlying relational structure as an inductive bias. This principle underlies foundational methods like Bayesian Networks~\cite{ankan2015pgmpy, zhang2017privbayes} and has been integrated into advanced deep learning frameworks such as DECAF~\cite{van2021decaf} and GOGGLE~\cite{liu2023goggle}. However, the robustness of these methods hinges on the accurate discovery of the dependency graph. In data-scarce scenarios, the estimation of this graph becomes unstable, thereby compromising the integrity of the generation process. More recently, \textbf{Large Language Models (LLMs)} have emerged as a powerful paradigm for few-shot generation, leveraging vast prior knowledge to seemingly bypass data scarcity~\cite{cllm2024}. However, LLMs process tabular data through linearized textual representations, which means they can only \textit{induce} dependencies sequentially from the input sequence rather than capturing explicit graphical relationships~\cite{liu2024rethinking}. This structure-blind approach often produces synthetic data that fails to respect the true underlying dependency structure. In conclusion, existing paradigms either fit dependencies \textit{implicitly} (data-hungry), assume reliable graph discovery (which collapses in low-data regimes), or \textit{induce} dependencies from unstructured text (structure-blind).

These observations motivate a \textbf{discover-then-synthesize} paradigm that decomposes tabular data synthesis into two distinct stages: (1)~\textit{dependency structure discovery}---mining stable relational patterns from limited samples while avoiding spurious correlations, and (2)~\textit{structure-conditioned generation}---synthesizing data that explicitly respects the discovered structure rather than merely inducing it from sequential input.
This framing leads to two fundamental research questions: 
\textit{(RQ1) How can we reliably discover the underlying dependency structure when data is limited?}
% reducing spurious dependencies and improving discovery stability?}
\textit{(RQ2) How can we ensure that the generation process explicitly follows this discovered structure to achieve high structural fidelity?}
Addressing these questions poses significant challenges: limited samples destabilize statistical association tests, making discovered dependencies prone to spurious correlations; and ensuring structural fidelity during generation requires more than sequential conditioning.

To address these challenges, we propose \textsf{StructSynth}, a decoupled two-stage framework for \underline{Struct}ure-aware tabular data \underline{Synth}esis, as illustrated in the lower panel of Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}.
In the \textbf{Dependency Structure Discovery} stage, we propose an LLM-guided breadth-first search that integrates statistical association cues 
% (Pearson's R, Cramér's V, and Correlation Ratio) 
with the model's prior knowledge to progressively construct a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG)\footnote{Here, we use ``DAG'' to denote a directed dependency graph: edges capture statistical/probabilistic dependencies inferred from limited observations and are used as a generative blueprint, not as a claim of ground-truth causality.}. A cycle resolution mechanism with explicit rationales ensures the graph remains acyclic while preserving the most plausible dependencies.
In the \textbf{Structure-Guided Synthesis} stage, we leverage this DAG as a structural blueprint: nodes are partitioned into topological layers, and data synthesis proceeds autoregressively with each feature conditioned explicitly on its parent nodes. This design guarantees, \textit{by construction}, that the synthetic data strictly adheres to the discovered dependency structure.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item We propose a \textbf{discover-then-synthesize} framework that decouples dependency structure mining from data generation, enabling reliable pattern discovery under severe data scarcity.
    \item We introduce an LLM-guided structure discovery mechanism that combines statistical cues with prior knowledge and employs reasoned cycle resolution, paired with a \textbf{structure-conditioned generation} stage that enforces dependencies by design through topological layering and parent-conditioned synthesis.
    \item Extensive experiments demonstrate that \textsf{StructSynth} achieves state-of-the-art downstream utility (average rank 1.00 across six datasets) while providing the best \textbf{privacy-fidelity trade-off}, proving its effectiveness in challenging low-data regimes.
\end{itemize}
